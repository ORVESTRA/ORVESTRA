## 1. Analytical Question

Where does Adobe capture value when generative AI makes image generation, video creation, document analysis, design assistance, and marketing content production increasingly accessible outside traditional professional software?

A second question follows:

If individual AI capabilities become easier to reproduce and foundation models become increasingly substitutable, can Adobe preserve value by owning the creative workflow, professional editing environment, enterprise content supply chain, customer data, governance, and path from generated output to production-ready asset?

## 7. Subscription Architecture

Adobe's economic foundation remains subscription.

The traditional relationship can be simplified as:

**User → Application Access → Recurring Subscription**

Major examples include:

- Creative Cloud
- Acrobat
- Adobe Express premium products
- Enterprise Experience products

Adobe generally recognizes subscription revenue over the term of the customer agreement.

This produces recurring and relatively predictable revenue.

However, AI introduces economic activity that can vary significantly by actual usage.

---

## 8. Subscription Plus AI Consumption

Generative AI requires variable computation.

One Creative Cloud subscriber may rarely use premium generative features.

Another may generate:

- Hundreds of images
- Video
- Audio
- Variations
- Large campaign asset sets

Adobe therefore increasingly combines subscription access with:

**Generative Credits**

and other forms of:

**Usage-Based Consumption**

Current Creative Cloud plans incorporate generative AI entitlements and credit structures, while enterprise AI products can use additional consumption models.

The architecture increasingly becomes:

**Subscription + AI Entitlement + Variable Usage**

[S5][S14]

## 9. AI-First Revenue

Adobe has introduced a metric referred to as:

**AI-first ARR**

This represents annualized recurring revenue associated with products and offerings Adobe identifies as AI-first.

At the end of Q2 FY2026, Adobe stated that AI-first ARR:

- Exceeded $500 million
- Had approximately tripled year over year

This is materially smaller than Adobe's total ARR of $27.10 billion.

The distinction should be preserved.

AI-first ARR measures a subset of Adobe's business.

It should not be interpreted as all revenue influenced by AI.

[S3]

---

## 10. AI-Influenced Versus AI-First

Adobe also uses the broader concept:

**AI-influenced ARR**

to describe recurring revenue associated with products in which AI contributes to customer value.

Adobe stated that by the end of fiscal 2025, AI-influenced ARR represented more than one-third of its overall book of business.

This is a much broader category than AI-first ARR.

Therefore:

**AI-First ARR ≠ AI-Influenced ARR**

The first measures newer AI-centered offerings.

The second reflects AI integrated into Adobe's broader installed product portfolio.

[S5]

---

## 11. Why This Distinction Matters

Photoshop does not become a completely new business simply because Generative Fill becomes important.

Acrobat does not become an entirely separate AI company because AI Assistant is integrated into the product.

AI can increase:

- Acquisition
- Retention
- ARPU
- Product utility
- Engagement

inside an existing subscription.

Therefore, Adobe can capture AI value without requiring every AI capability to become a standalone product.

This gives Adobe two monetization paths:

**Enhance Existing Subscription**

and:

**Create New AI-First Product or Consumption Revenue**

## 13. AI Disruption to Creative Software

Generative AI directly threatens parts of Adobe's traditional feature architecture.

Tasks that previously required:

- Manual masking
- Object creation
- Background replacement
- Retouching
- Illustration
- Resizing
- Composition work
- Stock search

can increasingly be performed through natural-language prompts.

This reduces the scarcity of some technical production skills.

It also reduces the value of some individual software features.

Adobe therefore faces a real cannibalization problem.

---

## 14. Feature Commoditization

AI can turn sophisticated functions into simple instructions.

Traditional workflow:

**Learn Tool → Locate Feature → Configure Parameters → Execute**

AI workflow:

**Describe Desired Outcome**

This can commoditize parts of the interface.

If a customer only values one isolated capability, a competing AI service may be sufficient.

Adobe therefore cannot rely entirely on:

**Feature Depth**

as the moat.

It increasingly needs:

**Workflow Depth**

---

## 15. Adobe's Response

Adobe's response is not to isolate AI from existing applications.

It is embedding AI throughout:

- Photoshop
- Illustrator
- Premiere
- Express
- Firefly
- Acrobat
- Enterprise marketing workflows

Adobe also introduced creative agents capable of orchestrating multi-step work across applications.

The strategy is effectively:

**Allow AI to simplify the tool while keeping the professional workflow inside Adobe.**

This is a form of controlled self-disruption.

## 24. From Tool to Outcome

**Ability to Reach a Desired Outcome**

## 26. Integration Versus Lock-In

**Photoshop → Illustrator → InDesign**

**Premiere → After Effects → Frame.io**

A marketing organization can connect:

**Creative Cloud → Firefly → GenStudio → Experience Manager**

The customer may remain because the connected workflow is useful.

The moat is partly:

**Accumulated Workflow Utility**

## 30. Generative Credits

Generative credits provide Adobe with a mechanism for connecting AI consumption to commercial value.

Traditional software economics:

**Subscription → Broad Product Access**

AI economics:

**Subscription → Product Access + Compute-Intensive Usage**

Credits allow Adobe to meter premium generative functions while preserving the recurring subscription relationship.

This creates a hybrid model:

**Recurring Subscription + Usage Allocation**

---

## 31. Why Credits Matter

AI workloads do not have identical cost.

Generating:

- A simple image
- High-resolution output
- Video
- Audio
- Multiple variants

can require materially different computation.

A credit architecture gives Adobe flexibility to price products around differential resource usage without replacing the entire Creative Cloud subscription system.

This is similar to the wider Group 2 movement from:

**Seat**

toward:

**Seat + Consumption**

- Third-party hosting services
- Data-center costs
- Network infrastructure
- AI inferencing costs

Adobe also states that research and development expenses include:

- Third-party hosting
- Data-center costs
- AI training costs

## 33. Subscription Cost Growth

For Q2 FY2026:

**Subscription cost of revenue increased 16 percent year over year.**

Adobe identified increased hosting services and data-center costs among the contributors.

Total subscription revenue increased:

**14 percent**

during the same period.

This should not be interpreted as proof that AI alone caused cost growth.

Hosting, infrastructure, support, royalties, and other factors are included.

But Adobe explicitly includes AI inferencing within the subscription cost structure.

[S2]

## 34. AI Training Economics

Adobe also bears compute costs before commercial inference occurs.

Training Firefly models requires:

- Compute
- Data infrastructure
- Engineering
- Model development
- Evaluation

These costs primarily appear through research and development rather than a separate AI training line.

Adobe does not publicly disclose:

- Firefly training cost
- Cost per generation

Therefore exact Firefly unit economics cannot be reconstructed from public disclosure.

## 35. Compute Without Hyperscale Ownership

Adobe differs structurally from Microsoft and Alphabet.

Adobe does not operate a hyperscale public cloud comparable with Azure or Google Cloud.

Its financial reporting identifies third-party hosting and data-center services as important operating inputs.

This gives Adobe a different AI architecture.

Adobe attempts to own:

**Creative Workflow + Model + Application + Customer Relationship**

while relying partly on external infrastructure for:

**Physical Compute**

This can reduce direct capital intensity relative to hyperscalers.

It also creates supplier dependency.

## 100. ORVESTRA Structural Observation

Adobe's central AI problem is not whether generative AI will disrupt creative software.

That disruption is already occurring.

The more important question is:

**Which layer remains economically scarce after generation becomes abundant?**

Adobe appears to be positioning around the answer:

**Workflow**

The company is allowing multiple models into Firefly.

It is making its own professional applications agent-accessible.

It is exposing creative capabilities through APIs.

It is turning creative production into enterprise workflows.

It is connecting content creation with marketing activation and customer data.

It is moving into AI-driven brand discovery.

This architecture suggests that Adobe is not betting solely on owning the generation model.

It is betting on owning enough of the path from:

**Idea**

to:

**Finished, Governed, Commercially Useful Output**

## 103. Analytical Limits

Adobe operates across multiple large product categories.

A single SaaS or AI metric cannot represent the entire business.

Beginning in fiscal 2026, Adobe reports one operating and reportable segment.

Historical Digital Media and Digital Experience figures remain useful for historical context but should not be treated as current reportable segments.

Total Adobe ARR is not equivalent to GAAP revenue.

AI-first ARR is not equivalent to AI-influenced ARR.

AI-first ARR should not be interpreted as all AI-related revenue.

The Q2 FY2026 ARR balance includes approximately $480 million associated with Semrush.

Q2 subscription revenue includes approximately $40 million associated with Semrush after the acquisition.

Firefly generation counts should not be interpreted as:

- Paid transactions
- Unique users
- Revenue

Creative freemium MAU should not be interpreted as paid Creative Cloud subscribers.

Partner-model economics are not publicly disclosed.

Adobe does not separately disclose:

- Firefly standalone revenue
- Firefly standalone profitability
- Firefly model gross margin
- Cost per generation
- Partner-model gross margin
- AI Assistant standalone profitability
- Creative-agent revenue
- Firefly Creative Production profitability
- AI inference cost per customer

Subscription cost of revenue includes multiple items beyond AI inference.

R&D expense includes multiple items beyond AI training.

This analysis does not estimate:

- Adobe AI standalone profitability
- Customer-level AI ROI
- Model-level margins
- Future Creative Cloud seat displacement
- Future creator market share
- Future partner-model economics
- Investment value

The analysis is limited to observable business architecture and publicly documented information.

## 104. Conclusion

Adobe entered the AI transition from an apparently vulnerable position.

Generative AI can perform tasks that historically required:

- Creative expertise
- Professional software
- Significant manual effort

That creates direct disruption to Adobe's traditional value proposition.

But Adobe also entered the transition with assets AI-native competitors do not automatically possess:

**Professional Creative Workflows**

**Installed Subscription Relationships**

**Creative File Infrastructure**

**PDF and Document Workflows**

**Enterprise Marketing Systems**

**Customer Data Platforms**

**Brand Relationships**

**Production and Approval Systems**

Adobe's strategy is increasingly clear.

It is not trying to keep AI outside Creative Cloud.

It is using AI to transform Creative Cloud.

It is not requiring every Firefly user to use an Adobe model.

It is turning Firefly into a multi-model environment.

It is not limiting Adobe capabilities to human-operated applications.

It is exposing them through APIs and agents.

It is not limiting monetization to seats.

It is introducing consumption and production-volume economics.

It is not limiting AI value to generation.

It is extending value toward editing, workflow, enterprise production, brand governance, activation, and measurement.

The result is a company attempting to move its moat from:

**Knowing How to Operate the Tool**

toward:

**Owning the System Through Which Professional Creative Work Becomes Production-Ready**

For ORVESTRA, the central observation is:

**If generative models make creation abundant, Adobe's durable value may depend less on owning the generation itself and more on owning the professional workflow that turns generated possibility into editable, governed, branded, and commercially usable output.**
