## 1. Analytical Question

How does Union Pacific convert fixed rail infrastructure into economic value?

A second question follows:

When freight demand increases, can Union Pacific move more volume through the existing network, or must it add:

- Track
- Sidings
- Terminals
- Locomotives
- Freight cars
- Crews

to create additional usable capacity?

Railroad economics differ fundamentally from parcel and air networks.

A truck can change highways.

An aircraft can be reassigned between routes.

A railroad can move locomotives and freight cars.

But its core transportation corridor is physically constrained by:

**Track Geography.**

The central Union Pacific architecture can therefore be simplified as:

**Customer Origin**

→ **Local Rail Access**

→ **Train Assembly**

→ **Mainline Corridor**

→ **Terminal / Interchange**

→ **Destination Corridor**

→ **Customer Destination**

Economic value depends on keeping freight:

**Moving Through Fixed Infrastructure**

with as little:

**Dwell**

**Handling**

**Congestion**

and:

**Unused Asset Time**

as possible.

Union Pacific Railroad is a Class I railroad operating across the western two thirds of the United States.

Its network connects:

- Pacific Coast ports
- Gulf Coast ports
- Midwest markets
- Eastern railroad gateways
- Canadian rail connections
- Mexican gateways
- Industrial centers
- Agricultural regions
- Automotive facilities
- Intermodal terminals

The company operates in:

**23 western states**

and is the only railroad serving all:

**Six major Mexico gateways.**

[S1]

Union Pacific's productive system consists of:

**Track**

**Yards**

**Intermodal Terminals**

**Locomotives**

**Freight Cars**

**Crews**

**Dispatching**

**Technology**

The railroad is therefore:

**A Fixed Corridor Network With Mobile Assets Operating on Top of It.**

## 3. Fixed Geography

Union Pacific's core network cannot be moved rapidly when demand changes.

A railroad corridor built between:

**Los Angeles**

and:

**Chicago**

cannot simply be transferred to another geography.

This creates a structural difference from:

- Trucking
- Parcel delivery
- Aviation

Rail infrastructure is:

**Location Specific.**

They should not be confused with total physical track miles.

## 6. Route Miles Are Not Capacity

A railroad with:

**More Route Miles**

does not automatically have:

**More Usable Capacity.**

Capacity depends on factors including:

- Number of main tracks
- Sidings
- Signal systems
- Terminal throughput
- Train length
- Locomotive power
- Crew availability
- Traffic mix
- Directional balance

Therefore:

**Network Size ≠ Network Throughput.**

---

## 7. The Track Is the Primary Constraint

Locomotives can be moved.

Freight cars can be moved.

Crews can be reassigned within operational constraints.

But trains must move through:

**Specific Rail Corridors.**

When one corridor becomes constrained, capacity elsewhere cannot automatically solve the problem.

This makes railway capacity:

**Geographically Localized.**

## 11. Carload Is Not Weight

Fiscal 2025 carloads increased:

**1 percent.**

**3 percent.**

This difference occurred partly because commodity mix changed.

---

## 12. Gross Ton Miles

Gross ton miles measure the weight of:

- Loaded freight cars
- Empty freight cars

multiplied by the miles hauled.

This captures the physical work performed by the railroad more directly than carload count alone.

A heavier train consumes more:

- Locomotive power
- Fuel
- Track capacity
- Infrastructure life

---

## 13. Revenue Ton Miles

Revenue ton miles measure:

**Revenue Freight Weight × Distance.**

This is another important activity measure.

It captures both:

**Weight**

and:

**Haul Length.**

One short-distance carload and one long-distance carload should therefore not be treated as equivalent network activity.

---

## 14. Route Economics Include Distance

Railroads incur substantial fixed infrastructure cost.

But each shipment also uses the corridor for a particular distance.

The economic problem is therefore:

**Revenue Per Shipment**

relative to:

**Weight × Distance × Network Resources Consumed.**

---

## 15. Revenue Carload Boundary

For intermodal shipments, Union Pacific counts:

**Each Container or Trailer**

as one carload for its revenue-carload reporting.

[S2]

This does not necessarily mean one physical rail car.

A rail platform can carry more than one intermodal unit depending on equipment configuration.

Therefore:

**Reported Intermodal Carloads ≠ Physical Rail Cars.**

## 22. Rail Competes With Highway Capacity

For many long-distance freight movements, customers can choose between:

- Railroad
- Trucking
- Intermodal combinations

Rail economics improve when it can concentrate large volumes into shared train movements.

Trucking offers greater route flexibility.

The competitive trade-off is therefore:

**Rail Density and Efficiency**

versus:

**Truck Flexibility and Directness.**

---

## 23. Bulk Economics

Bulk shipments include:

- Grain
- Fertilizer
- Food and refrigerated products
- Coal and renewables

Bulk can create high-density flows between:

**Large Origins**

and:

**Large Destinations.**

Selected traffic can be moved in highly consolidated train structures.

This can create efficient asset utilization when demand is sufficient.

---

## 24. Industrial Economics

Industrial traffic includes:

- Chemicals
- Plastics
- Metals
- Minerals
- Forest products
- Energy-related commodities

These movements can require more complex:

- Origin patterns
- destination patterns
- switching
- classification

than highly standardized train movements.

The number of car handlings can therefore become economically important.

---

## 25. Premium Economics

Premium includes:

- Automotive
- Intermodal

Intermodal creates an important bridge between:

**Rail Network**

and:

**Highway Network.**

The container can move:

**Truck → Rail → Truck**

without unloading the freight itself.

---

## 26. Intermodal Converts Highway Freight Into Rail Density

A truckload moving hundreds or thousands of miles can potentially be consolidated with many other loads onto one train.

The architecture becomes:

**Local Truck**

→ **Intermodal Terminal**

→ **Long-Haul Rail**

→ **Intermodal Terminal**

→ **Local Truck**

Rail provides the dense long-distance trunk.

Truck provides flexible endpoints.

---

## 27. Terminals Are Critical Nodes

Intermodal only works if terminals can efficiently transfer containers between:

- Road
- Rail

Union Pacific's major intermodal facilities include locations such as:

- Chicago
- Los Angeles
- Dallas
- Lathrop
- Memphis
- Laredo
- Houston

[S1]

Terminal capacity therefore determines how much highway demand can enter the rail network.

---

## 28. Terminal Capacity Is Not Track Capacity

A mainline can have available train paths.

But if:

**Intermodal Terminal Capacity Is Full**

additional traffic may still be constrained.

The reverse is also true.

A terminal can have spare capacity while the connecting mainline is congested.

Useful capacity requires:

**Node Capacity + Corridor Capacity.**

---

## 29. Classification Yards

Union Pacific also operates major classification yards.

These facilities:

- Receive freight cars
- Separate cars by destination
- Build trains
- Store cars temporarily
- Redirect traffic

The yard is therefore:

**A Physical Routing Engine.**

---

## 30. Classification Creates Flexibility

A manifest freight car does not need an entire train from one origin to one destination.

Cars from multiple customers can be combined.

At yards they can be:

**Reclassified**

into new trains.

This expands network reach.

---

## 31. Classification Also Creates Friction

Every additional handling can consume:

- Time
- Yard capacity
- Labor
- Locomotive resources

and increase:

- Dwell
- Delay
- Complexity

Therefore:

**More Network Connectivity**

can also create:

**More Handling Cost.**

---

## 32. Freight Car Velocity

Union Pacific reported 2025 freight car velocity of:

**225 daily miles per car**

up:

**8 percent**

from 2024.

[S1]

In Q2 2026, freight car velocity increased further to:

**231 daily miles per car**

up:

**5 percent**

year over year.

[S2]

---

## 33. Velocity Creates Capacity

A freight car that completes a journey faster becomes available for the next shipment sooner.

The same physical car can therefore support:

**More Transportation Cycles Per Year.**

Higher freight-car velocity effectively creates:

**Capacity Through Time.**

---

## 34. Capacity Without Buying Another Car

If freight cars move faster, Union Pacific may be able to move more freight without acquiring the same proportional number of additional cars.

The architecture becomes:

**Shorter Cycle Time**

→ **More Turns Per Asset**

→ **Higher Effective Capacity.**

This is one of the most important asset-utilization mechanisms in rail.

---

## 35. Terminal Dwell

Average terminal dwell declined to:

**20.9 hours**

in 2025.

That was an improvement of:

**8 percent**

from 2024.

[S1]

In Q2 2026, dwell improved further to:

**19.7 hours**

down:

**7 percent**

year over year.

[S2]

---

## 36. A Stationary Car Consumes Capacity

A freight car waiting in a terminal is not producing:

**Transportation Revenue.**

It is also consuming:

- Yard track
- switching capacity
- operating attention

Therefore dwell creates two costs:

1. Asset inactivity
2. Node congestion

---

## 37. Dwell Reduction Multiplies Through the Network

Lower dwell means:

**Cars Leave Faster**

→ **Yard Tracks Clear Faster**

→ **More Cars Can Enter**

→ **Freight Cars Return to Service Faster**

One improvement can therefore increase both:

**Terminal Throughput**

and:

**Fleet Utilization.**

---

## 38. Network Fluidity

Union Pacific explicitly describes:

**Freight Car Velocity**

and:

**Terminal Dwell**

as key indicators of network fluidity.

[S1]

A fluid railroad can move more traffic using existing infrastructure because assets spend less time:

**Waiting.**

---

## 39. Fluidity Is Capacity

This creates a critical Group 5 principle:

**Capacity Is Not Only Physical Infrastructure.**

Capacity can also be created through:

**Better Flow.**

A faster network can have more effective capacity without adding the same proportion of:

- Track
- locomotives
- freight cars

---

## 40. Train Speed

Average train speed was:

**24.3 mph**

in 2025.

It increased to:

**24.7 mph**

in Q2 2026.

[S1][S2]

The number may appear low compared with highway speeds.

But freight rail economics depend on moving enormous weight efficiently rather than maximizing top speed.

---

## 41. Speed Is Not the Only Service Metric

A train can move quickly between terminals but still produce slow end-to-end service if cars spend too long:

**Waiting in Yards.**

Therefore:

**Train Speed**

and:

**Terminal Dwell**

must be evaluated together.

---

## 42. End-to-End Cycle Time

The economically relevant sequence is:

**Train Movement Time**

plus:

**Terminal Time**

plus:

**Interchange Time**

plus:

**Customer Loading / Unloading Time**

The rail car only generates productive transportation when the entire cycle remains fluid.

---

---

## 44. Train Length Creates Capacity Per Movement

A train movement requires:

- Crew
- Locomotive power
- Mainline path
- Dispatching
- Terminal slots

If more freight can be placed on that same movement:

**Capacity Per Train Path Increases.**

This can improve economics.

---

## 45. Train Length Is Not Unlimited

Longer trains can create constraints involving:

- Sidings
- Yard tracks
- Terminal design
- locomotive power
- train handling
- geography

Therefore:

**Longer Is Not Automatically Better.**

The train must fit the physical network.

---

**Infrastructure Investment**

**Operating Design.**

## 49. Asset Count Is Not Productive Capacity

At year-end 2025, Union Pacific owned or leased:

**6,964 locomotives.**

[S1]

But only part of the fleet is actively required at any given time.

Therefore:

**Fleet Size ≠ Active Capacity.**

## 50. Locomotive Utilization

**68 percent.**

[S1]

**32 percent economically useless.**

The company deliberately maintains some equipment buffer.

## 51. Capacity Buffer

Union Pacific states that it maintains an equipment buffer because:

- Demand changes
- Weather changes
- Seasonality changes
- Equipment lead times can exceed one year

[S1]

Spare capacity therefore has:

**Option Value.**

---

## 52. Maximum Utilization Can Reduce Resilience

A railroad operating every locomotive continuously would have little flexibility when:

- Demand surges
- Equipment fails
- Weather disrupts operations
- routes change

Therefore the optimum is not:

**100 Percent Utilization.**

It is:

**Productive Utilization With Sufficient Resilience.**

**An Automobile Railcar**

**A Refrigerated Car.**

Rail capacity is:

**Equipment Specific.**

## 56. Right Asset, Wrong Location

A railroad can possess enough freight cars systemwide while still experiencing local shortages.

A car positioned in:

**California**

may not solve today's shortage in:

**Texas.**

Equipment repositioning consumes:

- Time
- track capacity
- locomotive capacity

Capacity therefore has:

**Geographic Position.**

---

## 57. Empty Moves

Freight cars sometimes must move empty to the next customer.

Empty cars still consume:

- Track capacity
- train length
- fuel
- locomotive power

while carrying no revenue freight.

Therefore network economics depend partly on:

**Directional Balance.**

---

## 58. Route Imbalance

If one corridor sends large volumes in one direction but has little return freight:

**Empty Repositioning Increases.**

A corridor with balanced traffic can use equipment more efficiently.

Thus:

**Outbound Volume Alone Does Not Define Route Quality.**

---

## 59. Revenue Density

The relevant economic quantity is not simply:

**Cars Per Train.**

It is closer to:

**Economic Value Carried Through Each Unit of Corridor Capacity.**

A route with dense, well-balanced, high-value traffic can produce stronger economics than one with large but poorly balanced flows.

Union Pacific must continuously replace:

- Rail
- Ties
- Ballast
- Signals
- Bridges
- Other road assets

before considering the full amount of capital available for growth.

This creates a fundamental difference from many digital networks.

## 69. Physical Capacity Versus Operating Capacity

A corridor has:

**Theoretical Physical Capacity**

and:

**Practical Operating Capacity.**

Poor scheduling can make a large physical network perform like a smaller one.

Better flow can make the same infrastructure support more freight.

**Terminal Capacity Is Full**

**The Constrained Layer.**

## 155. Analytical Limits

Union Pacific metrics require strict measurement separation.

Route miles should not be interpreted as total physical track miles.

Total track miles include mainline, passing, turnout, switching, and classification-yard track and should not be interpreted as unique geographic network distance.

Revenue carloads should not be interpreted as physical rail cars.

For intermodal reporting, each container or trailer is counted as one carload under Union Pacific's disclosed methodology.

Carload volume should not be interpreted as freight weight.

Gross ton miles and revenue ton miles measure different forms of transportation activity.

Average revenue per car should not be interpreted as profit per car.

Commodity-group revenue should not be interpreted as commodity-group operating profit because Union Pacific reports the integrated railroad as one operating segment.

Operating ratio is not a route-level or commodity-level margin.

The adjusted Q2 2026 operating ratio is a non-GAAP measure and should not be substituted for the reported GAAP operating ratio without preserving the adjustment boundary.

Freight car velocity should not be interpreted as train speed.

Train speed and terminal dwell are separate drivers of freight-car velocity.

Locomotive productivity should not be interpreted as locomotive utilization.

Freight-car and locomotive in-service utilization should not be interpreted as the percentage of equipment generating revenue at every moment.

Union Pacific deliberately maintains equipment buffers for resilience and changing demand.

Locomotive and freight-car fleet counts include both owned and leased equipment.

Spare equipment in one geography or equipment class should not be assumed to satisfy capacity requirements elsewhere.

Train length should not be interpreted as train weight or train revenue.

Longer trains should not automatically be interpreted as superior operating performance because physical infrastructure and service constraints differ by route.

The 2026 capital plan is a planned level of investment and should not be interpreted as fully realized spending.

The proposed Norfolk Southern transaction has not been approved or completed as of 13 September 2026.

Norfolk Southern routes, revenue, equipment, employees, and operating metrics should not be consolidated into current Union Pacific results.

The proposed more-than-50,000-route-mile network, 43-state footprint, approximately 100-port connectivity, 10,000 single-line lane conversions, synergy estimates, transit improvements, and other combination benefits are proposed or company-estimated transaction outcomes.

The Surface Transportation Board's acceptance of the revised application for consideration does not constitute approval of the transaction on its merits.

The STB removed the proceeding from abeyance in August 2026 and the regulatory and environmental review remained active as of this analysis date.

This analysis does not estimate:

- Corridor-level capacity
- Trains per corridor
- Siding utilization
- Yard utilization
- Terminal throughput
- Freight-car turns by commodity
- Empty repositioning ratio
- Route-level profitability
- Customer-level profitability
- Commodity-level operating margin
- Incremental carload contribution margin
- Proposed merger synergy realization
- Proposed merger integration success
- Future market share
- Investment value

The analysis is limited to observable business architecture and publicly documented information.

## 156. Conclusion

Union Pacific demonstrates one of the purest forms of physical-network economics.

The company's most important infrastructure cannot move.

Its:

**Track**

**Yards**

**Terminals**

**Gateways**

are embedded in geography.

That fixed network connects western ports, industrial centers, agricultural regions, major population centers, Eastern gateways, and Mexico.

The business therefore begins with a constraint:

**Freight Must Fit the Network.**

Union Pacific cannot move the railroad every time demand changes.

Instead, it must move:

**Equipment**

**Crews**

and:

**Operating Capacity**

around the railroad.

That makes network fluidity economically critical.

In 2025, freight-car velocity reached:

**225 miles per day**

while terminal dwell declined to:

**20.9 hours.**

By Q2 2026, freight-car velocity increased again to:

**231 miles per day**

and terminal dwell improved to:

**19.7 hours.**

Train length also increased.

Locomotive productivity increased.

Workforce productivity increased.

Carloads grew.

Those changes reveal the central mechanism.

Union Pacific created more effective transportation capacity not simply by buying:

**More Assets**

but by making existing assets spend:

**Less Time Waiting**

and:

**More Time Moving Freight.**

This matters because railroad infrastructure is expensive to reproduce.

The company invested approximately:

**$3.8 billion**

in capital during 2025.

Almost $2.0 billion went to road-infrastructure replacement alone.

The railroad must continuously spend money merely to preserve the physical network that creates its competitive advantage.

And increased utilization itself creates more wear.

That produces the central physical-network trade-off:

**Density Improves Economics**

but:

**Density Also Consumes Infrastructure.**

The company therefore needs enough traffic to spread fixed costs without creating congestion or destroying the reliability of the network.

The proposed Norfolk Southern transaction makes the same issue visible at continental scale.

Union Pacific argues that connecting the western and eastern networks under one operating structure can reduce interchange friction and create single-line routes.

But the network remains only proposed.

As of 13 September 2026, the Surface Transportation Board had moved the revised application into its merits-review process.

Approval had not occurred.

Norfolk Southern therefore remains outside Union Pacific's current operating network.

For ORVESTRA, the central observation is:

**Union Pacific's scarce asset is not the locomotive or the freight car. It is the fixed corridor network and the ability to keep freight moving through that network fast enough that the same physical infrastructure can produce more transportation capacity without proportional asset expansion.**
