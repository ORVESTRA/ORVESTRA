## 1. Analytical Question

How does UPS convert a large fixed physical network into economic value?

A second question follows:

When does additional package volume improve network economics, and when does it create low-quality utilization that consumes capacity without producing enough economic value?

UPS provides a useful answer because its business depends on coordinating:

**Packages**

→ **Pickup**

→ **Local Sort**

→ **Transportation**

→ **Hub**

→ **Linehaul**

→ **Destination Sort**

→ **Delivery Route**

The network contains significant fixed and semi-fixed infrastructure.

Its economics therefore depend not only on:

**How Many Packages Move**

but also on:

**Where They Move**

**When They Move**

**Which Service They Use**

**How Dense the Routes Are**

**How Much Capacity Must Be Maintained**

and:

**How Much Revenue Each Piece Produces**

---

## 2. Structural Summary

UPS operates an integrated physical logistics network spanning:

- Ground pickup and delivery
- Package sorting
- Air transportation
- Surface linehaul
- International gateways
- Customs brokerage
- Freight forwarding
- Contract logistics
- Healthcare logistics
- Cold chain

- Returns

The central operating asset is not one truck, aircraft, or sorting facility.

**The Coordinated Network Connecting Those Assets.**

## 3. Core Network Architecture

A simplified UPS package flow is:

**Shipper**

→ **Pickup Route**

→ **Origin Facility**

→ **Regional / National Transport**

→ **Hub / Sort**

→ **Destination Facility**

→ **Delivery Route**

→ **Recipient**

Each stage has:

- Capacity
- Labor
- Time
- Equipment
- Cost

A bottleneck at one stage can constrain the usefulness of capacity elsewhere.

## 6. Volume Is Not the Objective by Itself

A logistics network has substantial fixed or semi-fixed costs.

These can include:

- Facilities
- Aircraft
- Vehicles
- Technology
- Labor
- Maintenance

More volume can spread those costs across additional packages.

But this is only economically beneficial when the incremental volume produces enough revenue to cover:

- Handling
- transportation
- delivery
- labor
- capital consumption

Therefore:

**More Volume ≠ Automatically Better Network Economics.**

## 23. Density

Density is one of the most important economic properties of parcel logistics.

A driver may travel through the same neighborhood whether delivering:

**10 packages**

or:

**50 packages.**

When more economically valuable packages can be served within the same route geography, the cost of:

- Vehicle
- driver
- distance
- route time

can be spread across more revenue-generating pieces.

This is:

**Network Density Economics.**

---

## 24. Density Is Not Volume Alone

A million additional packages concentrated in:

**Dense Urban Routes**

can have different economics from a million packages spread across:

**Sparse Rural Routes.**

Therefore:

**Volume ≠ Density.**

UPS does not publicly disclose one standardized companywide route-density or stops-per-route measure suitable for direct external comparison.

---

## 25. Geographic Coverage Has a Cost

UPS provides service across a broad geographic footprint.

Broad coverage creates customer value.

But it can also require:

- Low-density routes
- Long driving distances
- remote delivery
- infrastructure duplication

The economic problem is therefore:

**Coverage**

versus:

**Density.**

---

## 26. Last-Mile Economics

The last mile is particularly important because one delivery driver and vehicle must physically travel to individual destinations.

Last-mile cost depends on variables including:

- Stops
- packages per stop
- distance
- traffic
- delivery location
- service commitment
- customer density

Network density therefore matters most where physical movement becomes highly distributed.

---

## 27. Packages Per Stop

A delivery to one address containing:

**Several Packages**

can have better economics than several separate stops each containing:

**One Package.**

The driver still incurs:

- approach time
- parking
- walking
- delivery confirmation

for each stop.

This explains why:

**Stop Density**

and:

**Package Density**

are related but not identical.

---

**UPS Owns Every Delivery Mile.**

**Owning More Infrastructure Is Not Automatically Better.**

- Customer relationship
- shipment information
- upstream transport

The correct structure depends on:

**Relative Density and Cost.**

**Physical Utilization**

**Economic Utilization**

## 61. Route Economics

**Route Revenue**

minus:

**Driver Cost**

minus:

**Vehicle Cost**

**Fuel**

**Allocated Network Cost**

**Distance and Time Must Produce Enough Revenue Density.**

## 62. A Route Is a Productive Asset

A route is not a physical asset in the accounting sense.

But economically it behaves like productive capacity.

The company allocates:

- driver
- vehicle
- time
- mileage

to a geographic sequence of deliveries.

Better route density increases the productivity of that capacity.

---

## 63. Last-Mile Scarcity

The scarce resource in last-mile delivery can be:

**Driver Time.**

A vehicle may have empty physical space.

But if the driver has no remaining route time, additional deliveries can still be difficult to serve.

Capacity therefore has multiple dimensions.

## 136. Analytical Limits

UPS financial and operational metrics require strict separation.

Average daily package volume should not be interpreted as unique customers.

Annual package volume should not be interpreted as revenue.

Average revenue per piece should not be interpreted as profit per piece.

Cost per piece should not be interpreted as the complete marginal cost of one incremental shipment.

Global package-volume metrics primarily relate to small-package operations and should not be treated as a measure of all Supply Chain Solutions activity.

U.S. Domestic and International average daily volume should not be compared with Supply Chain Solutions revenue as if they measure the same activity.

Ground, Deferred, and Next Day Air packages have different revenue and operating characteristics.

Package volume should not be treated as a direct measure of route density.

UPS does not disclose one standardized companywide route-density, packages-per-stop, or stops-per-route metric suitable for external comparison.

Facility count and square footage should not be interpreted as package throughput.

Vehicle count should not be interpreted as available delivery capacity.

Aircraft count should not be interpreted as available air capacity without considering operational status, maintenance, route assignment, and charter capacity.

The 2025 aircraft table included MD-11 aircraft that UPS stated were permanently grounded and retired during Q4 2025.

UPS's reported Q2 2026 GAAP operating results included material transformation and separation expenses.

Non-GAAP adjusted operating profit should not be treated as GAAP operating profit.

Amazon represented 10.6 percent of 2025 consolidated revenue, but revenue share should not be interpreted as package-volume share.

The reported reduction of more than 50 percent in UPS's largest-customer volume is relative to 2024 levels and should not be interpreted as a 50 percent reduction in total UPS package volume.

Reported 2025 network-reconfiguration cost savings are company-calculated program savings and should not be treated as direct incremental operating profit.

Closing a facility does not automatically mean all of its previous capacity disappeared; activity may be consolidated into other facilities.

The USPS Ground Saver arrangement covers a portion of relevant last-mile volume and should not be interpreted as outsourcing UPS's complete final-mile network.

UPS Healthcare revenue includes a portfolio of healthcare-related businesses and should not be treated as standalone parcel revenue.

The September 2026 global operating model represents a current organizational and operating architecture change. Its long-term financial effects are not yet established.

This analysis does not estimate:

- UPS route-level profitability
- Packages per stop
- Stops per route
- Aircraft load factor
- Vehicle utilization
- Facility utilization
- Incremental package contribution margin
- Customer-level margin
- Amazon-specific profitability
- Healthcare standalone operating margin
- Network density by geography
- Future network capacity
- Future market share
- Investment value

The analysis is limited to observable business architecture and publicly documented information.

## 137. Conclusion

UPS demonstrates why physical-network businesses cannot be understood by counting assets or counting volume alone.

In 2025, UPS moved approximately:

**5.2 billion packages.**

It operated:

- A large ground fleet
- Extensive package facilities
- A global air network
- International gateways
- Logistics facilities
- Specialized healthcare infrastructure

But the strategic issue was not:

**How Can UPS Put More Packages Into Those Assets?**

Instead, UPS deliberately reduced volume from its largest customer.

That decision created an immediate network problem.

Facilities did not disappear automatically.

Drivers did not disappear automatically.

Aircraft requirements did not disappear automatically.

Physical capacity had to be actively resized.

UPS therefore closed facilities, reduced workforce capacity, adjusted vehicles and aircraft, increased automation, and redesigned operating processes.

By Q2 2026, average daily package volume was still lower year over year.

But average revenue per piece was materially higher.

This demonstrates the central physical-network trade-off:

**Volume Creates Density.**

Density can improve economics.

But low-quality volume can consume scarce capacity faster than it creates value.

At the other extreme, removing volume without removing capacity can increase cost per piece because fixed and semi-fixed costs remain.

UPS therefore has to continuously align:

**Demand**

with:

**Capacity.**

The same logic appears at the route level.

A driver becomes more productive when economically attractive deliveries are concentrated inside the same geography.

A sorting facility becomes more valuable when sufficient volume passes through its infrastructure.

An aircraft becomes productive when route demand justifies its capacity.

But every layer can become either:

**Underutilized**

or:

**Constrained.**

The strongest network is therefore not the network with the most assets.

It is the network that can continuously decide:

**Which volume deserves scarce capacity, where that capacity should sit, and how much physical infrastructure the network actually needs.**

For ORVESTRA, the central observation is:

**UPS turns physical infrastructure into economic value through density, but density only becomes an advantage when the revenue quality of the traffic is sufficient to justify the capacity, labor, time, and route resources consumed by that traffic.**
