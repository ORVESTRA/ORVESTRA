## Structural Question

Where does Tesla's durable economic value actually sit:

**Manufacturing**

**Installed Base**

**Software**

**Charging Infrastructure**

**Energy Infrastructure**

or:

**AI-Enabled Fleet Services?**

Tesla is useful for Group 6 because it sits at the intersection of:

**Physical Manufacturing**

**Software**

**Energy**

**Infrastructure**

and:

**Artificial Intelligence.**

The central architecture can be simplified as:

**Manufacturing Capacity**

→ **Vehicle / Energy Product**

→ **Installed Base**

→ **Software + Services + Charging**

→ **Data**

→ **AI Capability**

→ **New Products and Fleet Services**

This creates a business model in which the initial physical product can become the distribution layer for additional economic activity after manufacturing is complete.

---

# 1. What Is the Actual Business?

Tesla appears to sell:

**Electric Vehicles**

but its actual operating architecture is broader.

As of 2026, Tesla generates revenue from:

1. Automotive sales
2. Automotive leasing
3. Automotive regulatory credits
4. Energy generation and storage
5. Used vehicles
6. Maintenance and collision
7. Paid Supercharging
8. Automotive insurance
9. Parts and merchandise
10. Software-related vehicle functionality
11. FSD subscriptions and related automotive ancillary sales

It is also investing heavily in businesses that remain earlier in commercialization or scale, including:

12. Robotaxi
13. Cybercab
14. Tesla Semi
15. Optimus
16. AI compute infrastructure
17. Semiconductor manufacturing capability
18. Expanded battery and material manufacturing

[S1][S2][S3]

The current business therefore remains primarily:

**Physical Product Manufacturing**

but the intended architecture is increasingly:

**Physical Product → Installed Base → Software / Service / Fleet Economics**

# 9. Switching Costs / Lock In

Tesla does not possess one universal hard lock-in mechanism.

A vehicle owner can buy a different brand next time.

But several softer switching mechanisms exist.

---

## Vehicle Ecosystem

A Tesla customer can become familiar with:

- Tesla app
- Supercharging
- Software interface
- FSD
- Service system
- Home charging

This can reduce friction for buying another Tesla.

It does not prevent switching.

---

## Software

FSD is tied to Tesla's vehicle and software architecture.

The customer's learned experience and subscription relationship can create ecosystem familiarity.

However:

**FSD Subscription ≠ Permanent Contractual Lock-In.**

---

## Energy Installed Base

Powerwall and energy systems can create deeper physical switching costs because they are installed into:

- Homes
- commercial sites
- energy systems

Replacing physical energy infrastructure is more complex than changing an application.

---

## Charging Network

Tesla's Supercharger network historically strengthened Tesla vehicle utility.

But opening more of the network to non-Tesla vehicles reduces its exclusivity.

This can simultaneously:

- Reduce vehicle lock-in
- Increase infrastructure monetization

The same strategic action can weaken one moat while creating another.

---

# 11. Disintermediation Risk

Tesla itself has already disintermediated several traditional automotive layers.

It generally sells directly rather than through independent dealer networks.

It distributes vehicle software through:

**Over-the-Air Updates.**

But Tesla can also be disintermediated.

---

## Hardware Commoditization

If EV hardware becomes highly standardized and multiple manufacturers can provide:

- Similar range
- Similar performance
- Similar charging access

economic value could migrate away from:

**The Vehicle Manufacturer.**

---

## Autonomy Could Move Outside the OEM

If autonomous-driving capability becomes:

**A Standard External Technology Layer**

that can be deployed across many vehicle brands, value could migrate from:

**Vehicle Manufacturing**

toward:

**The Autonomous Platform Provider.**

Tesla's strategy attempts to prevent this by controlling both:

**Vehicle**

and:

**Autonomy Stack.**

---

## Robotaxi Can Disintermediate Vehicle Ownership

Successful autonomous mobility could make:

**Private Car Ownership**

less necessary for some customers.

That creates an unusual internal disruption risk.

Tesla's Robotaxi strategy can potentially:

**Disintermediate Part of Tesla's Own Traditional Vehicle-Sale Model.**

Tesla appears to be attempting to own the new layer before it reduces the importance of the old one.

---

## Charging Disintermediation

As charging standards become more interoperable, Tesla vehicle owners may have more charging alternatives.

Meanwhile non-Tesla owners gain access to Tesla infrastructure.

Vehicle differentiation and infrastructure monetization can move in opposite directions.

---

# 12. Owned Distribution Risk

Tesla's direct model provides significant advantages.

Tesla controls:

- Vehicle pricing
- Customer ordering
- Digital sales
- Delivery experience
- Customer relationship

It does not need an independent dealer to represent the product.

---

## Direct Distribution Creates Responsibility

Tesla's 2025 Form 10-K states that because it lacks an independent dealer network:

**Tesla itself is responsible for delivering all vehicles to customers.**

[S1]

Therefore direct distribution transfers both:

**Control**

and:

**Operational Burden**

to Tesla.

## Delivery Can Become a Bottleneck

**Delivery Capability Must Also Increase**

or delivery can become a bottleneck.

[S1]

Manufacturing additional vehicles creates no economic benefit if they cannot be efficiently:

- Transported
- delivered
- serviced

## Service Is Part of Distribution Architecture

The installed vehicle fleet also requires:

- Service capacity
- parts
- body shops
- Mobile Service

[S1]

The physical product creates:

**Post-Sale Infrastructure Obligations.**

# 13. Human Behavior Change

Tesla's long-term business architecture depends on several possible behavioral changes.

---

## From Fueling to Charging

Electric vehicles change energy behavior from:

**Gas Station Refueling**

toward combinations of:

- Home charging
- Work charging
- Destination charging
- Fast charging

This changes the supporting infrastructure around the vehicle.

---

## From Static Product to Software Product

Customers increasingly expect vehicles to:

- Receive software updates
- add functionality
- improve digitally after purchase

The product becomes less static.

---

## From Software Purchase to Subscription

FSD increasingly creates:

**Recurring Software Economics**

around a durable physical vehicle.

At Q2 2026 Tesla reported:

**1.48 million active FSD subscriptions**

up:

**56 percent year over year.**

[S3]

Tesla also stated that more than:

**55 percent**

of new North American vehicle deliveries in Q2 included FSD subscriptions.

This is a company-reported attach-rate measure.

---

## FSD Boundary

Tesla explicitly states:

**FSD (Supervised) requires active driver supervision and does not make the vehicle autonomous.**

[S3]

FSD subscription metrics should not be interpreted as autonomous vehicles in commercial operation.

---

## From Ownership to Mobility Access

Robotaxi could change customer behavior from:

**Own Vehicle**

toward:

**Buy Transportation as Needed.**

This is potentially the most consequential behavioral shift for Tesla's business model.

---

## From Grid Consumption to Energy Management

Powerwall and Megapack support another behavior shift.

Electricity can increasingly be:

- Stored
- shifted across time
- used for backup
- integrated with intermittent generation

Tesla therefore operates not only in transport behavior but also in:

**Energy-Usage Behavior.**

# 15. What Could Make the Current Model Less Necessary?

This is the central Business Model Lab question.

Tesla's current model is still heavily dependent on:

**Manufacturing and Selling Physical Vehicles.**

Several structural changes could reduce the importance of that model.

---

## 15.1 Autonomous Mobility Could Reduce Private Vehicle Ownership

If autonomous fleets become:

- Reliable
- inexpensive
- widely available

some consumers may choose:

**Mobility Access**

instead of:

**Vehicle Ownership.**

That could reduce the structural importance of selling one vehicle to one household.

Tesla's strategic response is:

**Robotaxi + Cybercab.**

The company is attempting to participate in the model that could partially displace its existing one.

---

## 15.2 EV Hardware Could Become Commoditized

If many manufacturers offer:

- Comparable range
- Comparable battery life
- Comparable manufacturing cost
- Comparable charging access

vehicle hardware differentiation could decline.

Value may migrate toward:

- Software
- autonomy
- financing
- fleet operations
- services

Tesla would then need its non-hardware layers to carry more of the economics.

---

## 15.3 Autonomy Could Become Vehicle-Agnostic

If the leading autonomous-driving system becomes deployable across many vehicle manufacturers:

**Vehicle Manufacturing**

and:

**Autonomy Value Capture**

could separate.

Tesla's vertically integrated strategy depends partly on preventing that separation from making the Tesla vehicle layer economically secondary.

---

## 15.4 Charging Could Become Fully Interoperable

If charging access becomes universally available across:

- Tesla
- Other OEM networks
- Third-party networks

Tesla's charging advantage could become less important to vehicle purchase decisions.

However, Tesla could still capture value as:

**Charging Infrastructure Provider.**

---

## 15.5 Alternative Battery Technologies Could Shift Manufacturing Advantage

Tesla's manufacturing architecture depends substantially on lithium-ion battery technology.

Changes in:

- Chemistry
- Material supply
- Manufacturing methods

could shift cost advantage toward other suppliers or architectures.

Tesla's internal cell and materials investments are partly a response to this risk.

---

## 15.6 Energy Storage Could Become More Standardized

If utility-scale battery storage becomes highly standardized and interchangeable across suppliers:

**Megapack Hardware**

could face margin pressure.

Value could migrate toward:

- Grid software
- Financing
- integration
- energy-market participation

---

## 15.7 Distributed Energy Architecture Could Change Product Demand

Future electricity systems may rely on different combinations of:

- Grid-scale storage
- distributed batteries
- generation
- flexible load
- transmission

The mix determines which Tesla energy products remain structurally necessary.

---

## 15.8 Regulation Could Limit Autonomous Service

Robotaxi economics depend on the ability to operate autonomous fleets legally and safely.

If regulation materially limits:

- Deployment
- Geography
- Operating conditions

the service-based transition could occur much more slowly.

---

## 15.9 AI Economics Could Become Too Capital Intensive

Tesla's Q2 2026 capital expenditure outlook exceeded:

**$25 billion for 2026.**

[S2]

If autonomy, robotics, compute, semiconductor manufacturing, and fleet infrastructure require enormous continuing capital without sufficiently attractive monetization:

**The AI transition could reduce rather than expand economic returns.**

---

## 15.10 Tesla Could Successfully Make Its Own Current Model Less Necessary

This is the most important structural possibility.

Tesla could shift from:

**Selling Cars**

to:

**Operating Autonomous Mobility Assets.**

If successful, fewer private vehicle sales might be needed to generate the same or greater mobility output.

That would not necessarily mean Tesla failed.

It would mean:

**Tesla Changed the Unit It Monetizes.**

From:

**Vehicle Sold**

to:

**Miles of Mobility Delivered.**

That is the central potential business-model migration.

# ORVESTRA Structural Observation

Tesla's most important business-model question is no longer simply:

**Can Tesla Manufacture Electric Vehicles Profitably?**

It is becoming:

**Can Tesla use physical manufacturing to create an installed base that supports higher-value software, infrastructure, energy, and autonomous-service economics?**

The company already demonstrates several parts of that transition.

Vehicles distribute:

**FSD.**

The vehicle fleet creates:

**AI Training Data.**

The charging network creates:

**Infrastructure Economics.**

The installed fleet creates demand for:

**Service, Insurance, Parts, and Charging.**

Energy factories create:

**Grid Storage Capacity.**

But the transition creates a structural contradiction.

The more Tesla moves toward:

**Robotaxi**

the less certain it becomes that:

**Private Vehicle Ownership**

must remain the dominant unit of growth.

Tesla may therefore need to deliberately disrupt:

**Vehicle Sale Economics**

to create:

**Fleet Utilization Economics.**

That is not merely a new product.

It is a potential change in:

**The Unit the Business Monetizes.**

# Analytical Limits

Tesla's financial and operating metrics require strict separation.

Total automotive revenue includes automotive sales, regulatory credits, and leasing.

Automotive sales revenue can include software and connectivity-related components and should not be interpreted as pure vehicle-hardware revenue.

Tesla does not separately disclose complete FSD revenue or FSD gross margin.

FSD active subscriptions should not be interpreted as autonomous vehicles.

FSD (Supervised) requires active driver supervision.

Cumulative vehicle deliveries should not be interpreted as the currently active vehicle fleet.

Vehicle production should not be interpreted as vehicle deliveries.

Installed manufacturing capacity should not be interpreted as actual production rate.

Individual factory capacity figures should not automatically be summed into realized annual production capability without considering product mix, shared infrastructure, supply constraints, downtime, and operating conditions.

Q2 2026 Cybercab installed annual capacity above 125,000 units should not be interpreted as Cybercab annual production or deliveries.

Cybercab production beginning should not be interpreted as commercial fleet deployment at scale.

Tesla Semi was disclosed as commissioning in Q2 2026 and should not be represented as full-scale production.

Optimus manufacturing infrastructure was under construction and should not be represented as commercial mass production.

Tesla's semiconductor manufacturing project was in early construction and equipment-procurement stages and should not be represented as operational chip production.

AI compute installed capacity in MW should not be interpreted as compute utilization or AI output.

Supercharger station count and connector count measure different physical units.

Supercharger growth should not be interpreted as Tesla-only charging demand because the network increasingly serves non-Tesla vehicles.

Services and Other revenue combines several businesses and should not be interpreted as standalone Supercharger, insurance, service, or used-vehicle revenue.

Services and Other gross margin does not disclose the profitability of each component business.

Energy-storage deployments in GWh should not be interpreted as energy revenue.

Megapack production capacity should not be interpreted as deployed energy-storage capacity.

Energy-storage deployment timing can vary materially by project milestones and logistics.

Energy segment gross margin can be materially affected by:

- product mix
- manufacturing credits
- tariffs
- warranty
- battery costs

Robotaxi announced metro coverage should not be interpreted as equivalent regulatory status, operating conditions, fleet size, or service availability across each market.

A Tesla-disclosed metro labeled Ramping Unsupervised should not be generalized into unrestricted autonomous operation outside the disclosed service context.

Robotaxi operations should not yet be treated as a mature revenue stream comparable with automotive sales.

Tesla does not disclose standardized Robotaxi:

- Fleet utilization
- revenue per mile
- contribution margin
- customer acquisition cost
- fleet maintenance cost

Tesla does not disclose standardized Optimus:

- production cost
- selling price
- commercial deployment
- unit economics

This analysis does not estimate:

- Model-level gross margin
- FSD standalone gross margin
- Supercharger standalone margin
- Vehicle customer lifetime value
- Robotaxi unit economics
- Cybercab fleet economics
- Optimus economics
- Megapack project-level margin
- Vehicle factory utilization
- Battery-pack utilization
- AI compute utilization
- Future autonomous market share
- Future vehicle market share
- Investment value

The analysis is limited to observable business architecture and publicly documented information.

# Conclusion

Tesla opens Group 6 by demonstrating that the economics of physical technology do not necessarily end when the physical product leaves the factory.

Tesla produced approximately:

**1.65 million vehicles**

during 2025.

It delivered approximately:

**1.64 million.**

By the end of Q2 2026, cumulative deliveries had reached approximately:

**9.7 million vehicles.**

That physical installed base creates a second economic layer.

The vehicle can consume:

**Software.**

It can consume:

**Charging.**

It requires:

**Service.**

It can generate:

**Real-World Data.**

It can become:

**An Autonomous Fleet Asset.**

The initial manufacturing transaction can therefore become the beginning rather than the end of the commercial relationship.

Energy demonstrates the same architecture differently.

Tesla manufactures:

**Megapack**

and:

**Powerwall.**

Those products become long-lived physical infrastructure inside:

**Homes**

**Commercial Sites**

and:

**Electricity Systems.**

Yet the entire model remains constrained by physical reality.

Tesla identified battery-pack capacity as the primary near-term constraint on increasing vehicle production in Q2 2026.

The company is responding through:

- Cell manufacturing
- Cathode production
- Lithium refining
- Localized supply chains

AI creates another physical bottleneck.

Autonomy requires:

- Compute
- Data centers
- Electricity
- Semiconductors

Tesla is therefore moving vertically into another infrastructure stack.

The company is becoming more software-driven while simultaneously becoming:

**More Capital Intensive.**

This makes Tesla an especially important Business Model Lab case.

Software does not replace manufacturing.

AI does not eliminate physical infrastructure.

Instead:

**Software and AI increase the economic importance of the physical system capable of deploying them into the real world.**

The deepest structural question is therefore not whether Tesla remains an automotive company.

It is:

**Whether vehicle manufacturing becomes the final product or merely the distribution mechanism for a larger software, infrastructure, energy, robotics, and autonomous-service system.**

For ORVESTRA, the central observation is:

**Tesla's long-term business-model transition depends on turning manufacturing scale into an installed base, the installed base into data and recurring relationships, and those relationships into software, infrastructure, and fleet economics that can remain valuable even if the traditional one-customer-one-vehicle ownership model becomes less necessary.**
